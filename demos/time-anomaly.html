What just happened?

  • For every reading I compare it with the average and usual wobble of the recent past. If it is too far off, the alarm rings.
  • A low alarm line rings at everything, even normal wobbles. You catch the real problems, but most alarms are false.
  • A high line only rings for huge jumps. Every alarm is real, but the subtle problems slip by.
  • There is no perfect line: you are choosing which mistake you can live with. A smoke alarm should be jumpy; a spam filter should not.
  • Slow drifts are sneaky because my idea of normal drifts along with them. A longer memory helps.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is a rolling z-score detector: zt = (xt − μt) / σt, where μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of the previous w readings (never including the current one, so there is no peeking). Alarm if |zt| > threshold. The first w readings are a warm-up with no alarms.

  • Precision = alarms that were real problems ÷ all alarms. Recall = real problems that got at least one alarm ÷ all real problems. F1 is their harmonic mean. Raising the threshold generally raises precision and lowers recall.
  • For pure Gaussian noise, a threshold of 3 flags about 0.3% of normal points, a threshold of 2 about 5%, which at one reading per minute is a lot of false alarms.
  • The hidden labels are 8 big spikes, 4 subtle spikes and one slow drift (points at least 3.5 off). Recall counts each of these 13 events once. Precision counts individual alarms.
  • A rolling baseline adapts to concept drift, so a slow ramp can look normal. Real systems also use seasonal baselines, robust statistics (median and MAD) or learned models such as isolation forests and autoencoders.
  • Anomaly labels are rare in practice, so people often tune the threshold to a tolerable alarm rate rather than to F1.

Further reading: Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar (2009), Anomaly Detection: A Survey.
