What just happened?

  • Both robots use the same move: pick a stretch of the route and reverse it. If the route gets shorter, keep it.
  • The greedy robot refuses every bad move, so it gets stuck in a dip where any single change makes things worse.
  • The annealer sometimes accepts a worse route. That lets it climb out of dips. As it cools, it accepts fewer and fewer.
  • Cooling too fast freezes it in a poor route. Too hot at the end and it just wanders.
  • Neither is guaranteed to be perfect. The "best known" is the shortest of three long annealing runs.

How it works

  1. Start with a random route.
  2. Propose a small change: reverse a stretch of the route.
  3. If shorter, accept it.
  4. If longer, accept with chance e^(−extra / temperature). Hot means likely.
  5. Cool the temperature slowly towards zero.
  6. Keep the best route seen along the way.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

Simulated annealing is a stochastic local search inspired by cooling metal. It samples from a Boltzmann distribution over routes whose temperature T shrinks over time.

  • Move: 2-opt (reverse the segment between two cut points). Acceptance: the Metropolis rule, accept with probability min(1, e−Δ/T), where Δ is the change in route length.
  • Schedule: geometric cooling T(t) = T₀ (T_end / T₀)t/N. With a logarithmically slow schedule, annealing provably converges to the global optimum, but that is too slow to use; practical schedules are a trade-off.
  • The greedy robot is hill climbing (here, descending): it stops at a local optimum, a route that no single 2-opt move can improve.
  • The travelling salesman problem is NP-hard: the number of routes grows factorially, so we rely on heuristics. Here the reference value is only a very good heuristic answer, not a proof of optimality.

Further reading: Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi, Optimization by Simulated Annealing, Science (1983).
