What just happened?

  • A test is not the answer. It is a clue that moves what you believe.
  • Count the dots: of everyone flagged, the sick ones (red) and the false alarms (orange) are what matter.
  • When the thing is rare, even a small false-alarm rate makes lots of orange dots, so a positive is often a false alarm.
  • Each new result starts from your last belief. Two positives in a row are much stronger than one.
  • This is exactly how robots update: start with a guess, see evidence, move the guess.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is Bayes' rule: P(H | +) = P(+ | H) P(H) / [P(+ | H) P(H) + P(+ | ¬H) P(¬H)]. Here P(H) is the prior (prevalence), P(+ | H) is the sensitivity (true-positive rate), P(+ | ¬H) is the false-positive rate, and the result is the posterior.

  • The dot grid uses natural frequencies (Gigerenzer): counting people instead of multiplying probabilities makes the answer much easier to see. Posterior = red / (red + orange).
  • Base-rate neglect is the common mistake of ignoring the prior. With 1% prevalence, 90% sensitivity and 10% false positives, only about 8% of positives are real.
  • Repeated tests are applied sequentially: yesterday's posterior is today's prior. This assumes the tests are conditionally independent given the truth, which real repeated tests often are not (the same flaw can fool both).
  • In odds form it is simpler: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, where the positive likelihood ratio is sens / fpr.
  • Spam filters (the earlier lesson) and many classifiers use exactly this update, word after word.
