What just happened?

  • The robot learns only from the table it is given. A blank read as 0 or a height of 450 cm pulls its line far away from the truth.
  • The test dogs were clean and hidden, so the score shows how the line works on real dogs.
  • Dropping bad rows works but throws away data. Filling blanks keeps rows, and predicting a blank from the other column is usually the smartest fill.
  • The mean is dragged by wild values, the median is not. That is why outliers and fills are best handled together.
  • Clipping squeezes wild values into the normal range, but a typo is still a wrong number. Sometimes dropping it is more honest.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The model is ordinary least squares simple linear regression: weight = a · height + b with a = cov(x,y)/var(x). Score is R² = 1 − SSres/SStot on held-out rows, clamped at 0, and the typical miss is the mean absolute error.

  • Wild values are found with Tukey fences: values beyond Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQR. Clipping to the fences is called winsorising.
  • The typos here are decimal slips (a height ×10 or a weight ÷10); outliers are weights ×3 to ×6. Both are injected with a seeded random generator, so the same notebook is deterministic.
  • Mean imputation shrinks variance and pulls toward the centre; median imputation is robust to outliers; regression imputation fills a blank from the other column, which keeps the relationship but makes data look tidier than it is.
  • Leaving blanks as 0 is a classic silent bug: it is a legal number, so nothing crashes, but it is wrong.
  • Dropping rows is fine when blanks are random (missing completely at random). If blanks are linked to the answer, dropping them biases the model.
