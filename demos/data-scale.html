Challenges

See the problem: with no scaling and income in dollars, make age supply under 5% of the distance.
Fair fight: reach at least 85% test accuracy with scaling on, from a start under 75%.
Units matter: with no scaling, switch income to thousands of dollars and watch accuracy jump.
Faster learning: pick a scaling that gets the learning error below 1% of its start within 60 steps.

What just happened?

  • The robot measures how far apart two people are by adding up the gaps in each clue. A gap of 50,000 dollars is huge next to a gap of 30 years, so income wins and age hardly counts.
  • Scaling squeezes every clue onto a similar size. Then age and income both get a vote and the nearest people really are the most alike.
  • Writing income in thousands of dollars helped too. The units you pick quietly change what the robot sees.
  • Learning by small steps (gradient descent) has the same trouble: with clues of very different sizes it creeps along. Scaled clues learn much faster.
  • Not every robot cares. Decision trees ignore sizes. But distance-based and step-by-step learners need fair sizes.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The classifier is k-nearest neighbours (k = 5, majority vote, Euclidean distance) and the data is two features with labels from age_norm + income_norm + noise > 1. Accuracy is measured on 300 held-out people; scalers are fitted on the 200 training people only to avoid data leakage.

  • Min-max scaling: x' = (x − min) / (max − min), mapping to [0, 1] but sensitive to outliers.
  • Standardisation (z-score): x' = (x − μ) / σ, giving mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
  • Euclidean distance is √Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)², so a feature with a larger range dominates the sum. The "distance share" bars show Σ(Δage)² / Σ(Δ²) over the neighbours.
  • The learning-speed chart fits spend = w₁·age + w₂·income + b by gradient descent on mean squared error, always using the largest stable step 1/λmax of the Hessian (2/n)XᵀX (found by power iteration). The loss surface is a long thin valley when features differ in scale (a large condition number), so progress along the flat direction is very slow. Scaling reduces the condition number.
  • Used with scaling: k-NN, k-means, SVMs, PCA, neural networks, regularised regression. Mostly unaffected: decision trees and random forests.
