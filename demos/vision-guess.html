What just happened?

  • Your picture was squished to 224 × 224 pixels and turned into about 150,000 numbers.
  • Those numbers flowed through a stack of layers that found edges, then parts, then whole things.
  • The last layer gave a score to 1,000 things, and Robo told you the top 5.
  • Covering parts of the picture showed where Robo looked to make up its mind — and sometimes it looks at surprising places!
For grown-ups: what's really going on
  • The model is MobileNet v2 (α = 1.0, 224×224 input), a convolutional neural network trained on ImageNet-1k, running in-browser with TensorFlow.js (WebGL backend when available). It uses depthwise-separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks to stay small (~3.5M parameters).
  • We centre-crop your image to a square, resize to 224×224, scale pixels to [−1, 1], and take the softmax over the 1000 class logits. The bar chart in "Last layer" is that full probability vector, in class-index order.
  • "Where does Robo look?" is occlusion sensitivity (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014): slide a gray patch with 50% overlap, re-run the network on each occluded copy (batched), and record the drop in the top class's probability. It's model-agnostic and honest, but coarse; gradient methods like Grad-CAM give finer maps.
  • The "Edges" panel is a Sobel filter on your image, shown as an analogy: real first-layer MobileNet filters learn similar oriented-edge and colour-blob detectors.
  • Known limits: ImageNet is photo-centric with many dog breeds (~120 of 1000 classes) and no "person" class, so drawings and people get odd guesses. Dataset bias shows up directly in what it can and can't name.
