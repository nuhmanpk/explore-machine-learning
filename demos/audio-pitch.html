What just happened?

  • A note is a wiggle that repeats. The faster it repeats, the higher the note. A4 repeats 440 times every second.
  • Real instruments add overtones: extra wiggles at 2, 3, 4… times the note. The rainbow bars show each one. Their mix is what makes a flute sound like a flute.
  • The biggest-bar ear just names the loudest bar. When an overtone is louder than the note itself (violin) or the note is missing, it jumps to the wrong note, often a whole octave too high.
  • The echo ear asks a cleverer question: how long until the wiggle repeats? The overtones all repeat together with the note, so it still finds the right one, even with the bottom note taken away. Your own ears do this too!
  • Hiss is random, so it never lines up with itself. That is why the echo test shrugs it off.

How it works

  1. Robo builds the sound out of numbers: one sine wave for the note, one for each overtone, plus random hiss. 16,000 numbers make one second.
  2. It splits 4,096 of those numbers into a rainbow of pitches with a fast maths trick called the FFT. Each bar is about 4 Hz wide.
  3. Biggest-bar ear: pick the tallest bar between 60 and 2,400 Hz.
  4. Echo ear: compare the wiggle with a slid-along copy of itself, find the first slide where it lines up well, and turn that time into a pitch. Then fine-tune it using the exact spots of the overtone bars.
  5. The note name and the cents come from counting keys away from A4 = 440 Hz.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

Everything here is computed in plain JavaScript on generated sample arrays (fs = 16 kHz, frame N = 4096), so it runs identically in Node. Web Audio is used only to play the sound and, if you opt in, to read the microphone.

  • Spectrum. The frame is multiplied by a Hann window w[n] = ½ − ½cos(2πn/(N−1)) to reduce spectral leakage, then transformed with an iterative radix-2 Cooley–Tukey FFT (O(N log N); checked against a naive O(N²) DFT). Bin resolution is fs/N ≈ 3.9 Hz, so a peak is refined by fitting a parabola to the log magnitudes of its three bins.
  • Peak picking returns the loudest bin. It is wrong whenever harmonic 2 or 3 outweighs the fundamental (our violin preset) or the fundamental is absent, giving classic octave errors.
  • Autocorrelation r(τ) = Σ x[n]x[n+τ] is computed via the FFT (Wiener–Khinchin: inverse transform of |X|², zero-padded to avoid wrap-around), made unbiased by dividing by N−τ and normalised by r(0). We take the first local maximum (parabola-interpolated) whose height is at least 85% of the global maximum within 60–1100 Hz; picking the first such peak avoids sub-octave errors. Because every harmonic of f0 is periodic in 1/f0, the peak sits at the true period even when the fundamental is missing. White noise is uncorrelated with itself at non-zero lags, so it mainly lowers the peak rather than moving it.
  • Fine tuning. Lag resolution is coarse at high pitch (1 sample at 16 kHz is ~10 cents at 440 Hz), so the autocorrelation estimate is refined by locating each visible harmonic's interpolated peak near h·f0 and averaging fh/h, weighted by power. Typical error: under 1 cent clean, a few cents at 0 dB SNR.
  • Harmonic product spectrum (the "third opinion"): HPS(f) = Πh=1..5 |X(hf)|, computed as a sum of logs with a floor so a single missing harmonic is a penalty rather than a zero, with ties broken towards the higher candidate. It handles the missing fundamental well but can still be fooled, for example by the buzzy robot with its fundamental removed.
  • Cents: 1200·log2(f/fref), with equal temperament and A4 = 440 Hz (MIDI note 69, f = 440·2(m−69)/12). 100 cents is one semitone; trained listeners notice about 5–10 cents.
  • Spectrogram: a short-time Fourier transform (1024-sample Hann frames, hop 256), drawn on a logarithmic frequency axis like a piano. The pitch track runs the same autocorrelation detector on each frame, skipping quiet or unvoiced frames.

The "missing fundamental" is a real perceptual effect: you hear the pitch of a tone whose fundamental has been removed, which is why small phone speakers can still play bass lines. Production pitch trackers such as YIN, pYIN and the neural CREPE build on exactly these ideas; speech and music systems often feed log-mel spectrograms (like the strip above) into neural networks.
