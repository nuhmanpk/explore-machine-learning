How it works

Old decisions

The AI is shown lots of creatures and whether the old bouncer let them in.

Find the clues

It works out which clues (colour, size, hat, snack) went with "let in".

Copy the pattern

It copies the pattern it saw, fair or not. It can't tell the difference.

Check every group

A good score overall can hide a bad score for a small group, so we check each group on its own.

Fix the examples

To fix an unfair AI, fix what it learns from: the examples, the labels, or what it's allowed to look at.

What just happened?

  • The AI looked great on the old decisions, but a fair test showed purple creatures were treated badly. A single score can hide unfairness.
  • Nobody told the AI to dislike purple. It just copied the old bouncer, because purple creatures were rare and often turned away.
  • Adding fairly-judged examples, hiding colour, and fixing the old decisions all helped. Copying the same unfair examples did not, because the copies carry the same unfairness.
  • AI is only as fair as the examples it learns from. People have to check, and fix.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The bouncer is a logistic regression trained by full-batch gradient descent with a little L2 decay on features [purple, big, hat, snack] plus a bias. This is a deliberately small, transparent demonstration of dataset bias, not a claim about any real system.

  • The two problems baked into the data: (1) representation: only 10% of the 160 examples are purple; (2) label bias: the old bouncer let in purple creatures that brought a snack only 15% of the time, against about 96% for green creatures. The fair rule ("let in anyone with a snack") is the ground truth used by the fair test.
  • Why one number misleads: accuracy on held-out old decisions (drawn from the same biased process, 10% purple) is about 94%. On a balanced fair test it is 100% for green creatures and about 48% for purple. The summary metric is dominated by the majority group.
  • Fairness meter: this is an equal-opportunity style check: among creatures that genuinely qualify (brought a snack), how confident is the model that they should be let in, per group? We show the average predicted probability because the hard 0/1 decision flips abruptly.
  • Add fairly-judged examples: collecting more, correctly labelled data for the under-represented group. The colour weight shrinks as evidence accumulates.
  • Hide the colour: "fairness through unawareness". It works here because no other feature is a proxy for colour. In real data, other features often leak the same information, so this is rarely enough.
  • Balance by copying: oversampling duplicates adds no new information and, with biased labels, makes the model more confident in the bias (the fair-test gap grows). Resampling helps with imbalance, not with label bias.
  • Fix the labels: relabelling the unfairly rejected examples removes the bias at its source. Real projects do this with audits, relabelling and careful data collection.
  • Fairness has several competing definitions (demographic parity, equalised odds, calibration) that cannot all be satisfied together in general. Further reading: Google's Responsible AI practices, Fairness and Machine Learning (Barocas, Hardt, Narayanan).
