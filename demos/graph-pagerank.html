What just happened?

  • The surfer follows a random link, but now and then gets bored and jumps to any page. Pages with many good pages linking to them are visited more.
  • The visit counts settle towards a fixed set of shares. Those shares are the PageRank of each page.
  • The orange bars get the same answer without any surfer: each page repeatedly shares its score among its links until nothing changes.
  • Adding links to a page raised its score. A link from an important page is worth more than one from a nobody.
  • A link farm helps for a while, which is why real search engines work hard to spot and ignore these tricks.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

PageRank (Brin and Page, 1998) is the stationary distribution of a Markov chain on the link graph. With damping factor d and N pages: PR(j) = (1 − d)/N + d · Σi→j PR(i) / outdeg(i).

  • The random surfer follows a uniformly random out-link with probability d and teleports to a uniformly random page otherwise. Teleporting makes the chain ergodic, so a unique answer exists.
  • Power iteration multiplies the current score vector by the transition matrix repeatedly. It converges geometrically at a rate of about d, which is why smaller damping settles faster. Pages with no out-links (dangling nodes) spread their score evenly over all pages.
  • The surfer's visit frequencies converge to the same vector by the ergodic theorem, with error shrinking like 1/√steps. This is a Monte Carlo estimate of the exact answer.
  • A link farm manufactures in-links. Real engines combine PageRank with hundreds of other signals, TrustRank-style seeds of trusted pages, and spam detection, so this simple version is easy to game and real ones are not.
  • The same idea ranks nodes in any graph: papers, accounts, roads, even proteins.
