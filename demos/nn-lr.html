    How it works

    Feel the slope

    The ball checks which way is downhill and how steep it is right where it stands.

    Take a step

    It moves downhill. The step size decides how far: steeper ground means a longer hop.

    Repeat

    Look, step, look, step. Every step should put it a little lower.

    Add some speed

    A heavy ball keeps some of its old speed, so it can zoom along valleys and roll over small dips.

    What just happened?

    • The ball always stepped downhill. The only thing we changed was how big the step was.
    • Tiny steps are safe but slow. Giant steps overshoot, bounce, or even fly away.
    • In a narrow valley plain balls zig-zag. A heavy ball, with a bit of speed, goes straight along it.
    • Starting big and getting smaller lets a ball move fast first, then settle gently.
    For grown-ups: what's really going on

    The landscape is a loss function L(w); the ball position is the parameter vector w, and each step is gradient descent: w ← w − η∇L(w).

    • Learning rate η: the slider. On a quadratic with curvature λ the iteration is stable only for η < 2/λ; above that each step overshoots by more than it started (divergence). That is the "flew away" ball. Below it, convergence speed is roughly 1 − ηλ per step, so a tiny η crawls.
    • Narrow valley: the demo valley has curvature about 0.1 along the floor and 3 across it (ill-conditioned, condition number ~30). η is capped by the steep direction, so the flat direction is slow, and large η zig-zags.
    • Momentum (heavy ball): v ← βv − η∇L; w ← w + v with β = 0.9 to 0.95. Gradients that agree accumulate (speed along the valley); ones that flip sign cancel (less zig-zag). It can also carry the ball through shallow local minima, as in the bumpy field.
    • Schedules: "Slowing down" uses η_t = η₀ / (1 + 0.04t) (inverse-time decay). Large early steps for progress, small late steps to settle; cosine and step decay are common alternatives.
    • Adam-lite: keeps running averages of the gradient (m) and its square (v) with bias correction, stepping η·m̂/(√v̂+ε). Each parameter gets its own effective step size. Real Adam is the same idea.
    • SGD noise: here gradients are exact (full batch). With mini-batches the gradient is noisy; the noise helps escape sharp minima but means a constant η never fully settles, hence decay schedules.
    • Real losses have far more dimensions, with saddle points (flat but not minima) more common than bad local minima. The step-size lesson is the same: tune η first.
    • Further reading: Goh, Why Momentum Really Works · Ruder, An overview of gradient descent optimization algorithms.
