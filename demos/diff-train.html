How it works

Make a mess on purpose

Take dots from the real shape and shake each one with a random push. Remember the push!

Guess the push

Show the brain the shaken dot and how hard we shook. It guesses which way the push went.

Fix the brain a little

Compare the guess with the real push. Nudge the brain's numbers so next time the guess is a bit closer.

Make something new

Start from pure fog. Ask the brain "which way was the push?", step the other way, and repeat 100 times.

What just happened?

  • At first the brain was brand new, so its dots stayed as random fog.
  • It only ever practised one tiny job: guess the noise on shaken-up dots.
  • The oops score dropped, and each checkpoint looked more like the real shape: fog → blob → shape.
  • To make dots, it starts from fog and removes a little guessed noise at a time. That's diffusion!
  • Picture-making AIs do exactly this, with millions of pixels instead of two numbers per dot.
For grown-ups: what's really going on
  • Real training, in your browser. A plain-JavaScript MLP (no libraries) is trained live with backprop and Adam (batch 128, gradient clipping at 1).
  • Objective. The DDPM "simple" loss from Ho et al. 2020: sample x₀ from the dataset, t ~ U{0..99}, ε ~ 𝒩(0, I), form xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ) ε, and minimise ‖ε − ε̂θ(xₜ, t)‖². Linear β schedule 1e-4 → 0.08, T = 100.
  • Network. Input is (x, y), Fourier features sin/cos(k·x) for k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8}, and a sinusoidal timestep embedding (34 numbers). Then 3 ReLU hidden layers of 32 / 64 / 128 units and 2 outputs (ε̂). The loss never reaches 0: even a perfect model can't know the exact noise, only its conditional mean, so the floor is well above 0, especially at high t.
  • Sampling. Ancestral DDPM: xₜ₋₁ = (xₜ − βₜ/√(1−ᾱₜ) · ε̂) / √αₜ + σₜ z, with σₜ² = β̃ₜ. With fewer cleaning steps we use strided DDPM steps (a respaced schedule), as in Nichol & Dhariwal 2021. The arrows show −ε̂ (the score direction, since ∇log pₜ = −ε̂/√(1−ᾱₜ)).
  • Checkpoints. At steps 0, 25, 75, 150 … the weights are snapshotted and 500 samples are drawn. The bar is the overlap between the sample histogram and the data histogram, divided by what genuine data samples score.
  • Inspired by tiny-diffusion. See the Diffusion Explainer (Polo Club) for the same process inside Stable Diffusion, where ε̂ comes from a U-Net working on image latents and is guided by text.
