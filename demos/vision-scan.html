How it works

Slide a window

A 3 by 3 window visits every spot of the picture.

Multiply and add

Pixel times scanner number, nine times, then add them up. That is the spot's score.

Compare to the answer

The robot checks how far its score map is from the right one.

Nudge the nine numbers

Small nudges, again and again, until the maps match.

What just happened?

  • A scanner is just nine numbers that slide over the picture, the same everywhere.
  • Where the picture looks like the scanner's pattern, the score is high.
  • Nobody told the robot what a vertical line is. It only saw the answer map and nudged its numbers.
  • Squashing the map keeps the strongest hits and makes it smaller.
  • Because the same scanner slides everywhere, it finds the pattern even when the picture moves.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is a single 2D convolution layer with one kernel (filter). Like most deep-learning libraries, it actually computes cross-correlation: y[i,j] = Σₐ Σ_b x[i+a, j+b]·w[a,b], without flipping the kernel. The output grid is a feature map. Here there is no padding (a "valid" convolution, so 12x12 becomes 10x10) and the stride is 1.

  • Training minimises MSE = mean (y − t)² over every position of several random pictures. The gradient is ∂MSE/∂w[a,b] = mean 2(y−t)·x[i+a, j+b], and the update is plain gradient descent: w ← w − η∇. The loss is quadratic in w, so a small enough η gives a steady decrease (the chart is on a log scale).
  • The hidden kernels are Sobel-style edge detectors and a corner detector. A real CNN is not told the answer; it learns kernels that reduce a task loss, and the kernels often end up looking like edges.
  • Max pooling (2x2, stride 2) keeps the strongest response in each block, shrinking the map and making it tolerant of small shifts.
  • Weight sharing gives translation equivariance: shift the input and the feature map shifts with it. Pooling and a final classifier turn this into approximate invariance.
  • A CNN stacks many such layers (with many kernels per layer, a nonlinearity such as ReLU, and pooling). Early layers find edges, later layers combine them into shapes and objects. This lesson is one kernel from one layer.
