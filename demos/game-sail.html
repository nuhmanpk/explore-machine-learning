How it works

No sailing into the wind

Point straight at the wind and the sail just flaps. The boat stops. Point a bit to the side, and it zooms!

What a boat feels

Where the wind comes from, where the buoy is, how fast it goes, how close the shore is, and 5 feelers for islands.

A tiny brain picks a course

It says "go 45° right of the wind" or "45° left". An autopilot turns the rudder to hold it.

Discover zig-zag

Boats that switch sides before the shore get closer to the buoy. Their brains get copied. That's how tacking is discovered.

What just happened?

  • In round 1 boats pointed at the buoy, stalled, or crashed into the shore.
  • After a few rounds the best boats zig-zagged: left of the wind, then right, then left, all the way to the buoy. Real sailors call that tacking.
  • Nobody taught them. Boats that happened to switch sides earlier did better, and their brains were copied.
  • Some challenges barely bothered them, because they already think in "angle to the wind". Others (islands, a moving buoy, current) broke their plan, and they had to adapt.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

Neuroevolution of a small tanh network [14 → 8 → 1] over a population of 60, using the same genetic algorithm as the river boats (elitism, 30% crossover, spread Gaussian mutation).

  • Sailing model: target speed follows a polar diagram: 0 inside the ±38° no-go zone, rising to full speed at 75–110° off the wind, easing off downwind. Speed eases toward the target (boats coast through a tack), plus leeway drift and optional current.
  • Action space: the network outputs a desired course relative to the wind (−100°…+100°); a proportional autopilot steers to it. Choosing where to point rather than raw rudder made tacking evolvable: with direct rudder control the population never found it.
  • Inputs: sin/cos of the buoy bearing and the wind direction relative to the heading, speed, distance, current, position across the channel and 5 ray-cast distances to islands and shores.
  • Fitness: reaching the buoy scores 2 + time bonus; otherwise mostly the furthest progress along the start→buoy line, so one long diagonal earns something, but crashing (×0.5) or stalling in irons (×0.7) is penalised. The channel is deliberately narrow, which makes a single diagonal a poor strategy and rewards tacking.
  • Robustness vs adaptation: because inputs are relative to the wind, wind shifts and gusts are a mild distribution shift; islands and a moving buoy change the task itself, so performance drops sharply and must be re-learned.
