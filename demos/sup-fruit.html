How it works

Examples with answers

Every fruit you drop comes with a name tag. The name tag is the "answer".

Turn fruit into spots

Roundness goes left-right, yellowness goes up-down. Every fruit becomes a spot on the table.

Ask the neighbours

For a new fruit, find the K fruit sitting closest to it.

Most votes wins

Each neighbour votes for its own name. The name with the most votes is the guess!

What just happened?

  • You gave the robot examples, each with the right answer.
  • The robot didn't do any hard thinking — it just remembered every example.
  • To guess a new fruit, it asked the closest examples and counted their votes.
  • More examples made the map smarter and the test score went up.
  • Asking 1 neighbour is jumpy; asking many is smoother but can miss small groups.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is k-Nearest Neighbours (k-NN) classification with two features (roundness, yellowness) and three labels.

  • k-NN is a lazy / instance-based learner: "training" is just storing examples. Prediction finds the k closest stored points by Euclidean distance √((x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²) and takes a majority vote.
  • The tinted background is the decision boundary; colour strength shows how unanimous the vote is.
  • k trades variance for bias: k = 1 memorises noise (jagged islands), large k smooths everything and can out-vote small classes. Odd k avoids ties with two classes.
  • The test score is accuracy on a held-out test set drawn from the same distribution; the chart is a learning curve (accuracy vs. training-set size).
  • In real use, features must be on comparable scales (normalise them), and k-NN gets slow and weak in high dimensions (the curse of dimensionality).
