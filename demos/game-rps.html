Rock, Paper, Mind Reader

Let's play rock, paper, scissors! I always lock in my move before you choose, so I can't cheat. My only trick is spotting your habits. Press R, P or S to start!

How to play

  1. Pick Rock, Paper or Scissors (buttons, or keys R P S).
  2. Robo has already locked its move in the box. Then both are shown.
  3. Play 20 rounds. Is Robo's line on the chart above the dashed 33% line? Then it is reading you!
  4. Send in a robot player, then try the challenges.

Who plays Robo?

Each robot player has a habit, except one. Tap one and press Play to watch Robo learn it.

Challenges

1. Teach Robo a habit

Play the same way on purpose (always rock, or rock-paper-scissors in order). Get Robo to win 70% of your last 20 rounds. Robo has won 0 of your last 0. Try this: now suddenly change your habit and count how many rounds Robo needs to catch on.

2. Crush the Cycler

Send in the Cycler robot. Watch Robo win 80% of 20 rounds in a row against it. Try this: switch to Copycat. Which spotter does Robo start trusting in "What's happening inside"?

3. Be hard to read

A 30-round test, no peeking. Keep Robo's wins under 40% (11 wins or fewer). Try this: look at "same again" in the Win / lose table. A dice repeats a third of the time. People usually repeat less, and Robo can spot that!

4. Why can't Robo beat random?

Send in the Truly random player for 60 rounds, then answer Robo's question.

How it works

Remember

Robo writes down every round: what you played, what it played, and who won.

Five spotters

Each spotter looks for one kind of habit: a favourite move, what you play after a move, after two moves, after winning or losing, and whether you copy Robo.

Trust who's right

Spotters that guessed right lately are trusted more. Robo mixes their guesses into one.

Lock in

Robo locks in the move that beats its best guess, before you choose. Old rounds slowly fade, so new habits get noticed.

What just happened?

  • At first Robo knew nothing, so it won about 1 round in 3, just like guessing.
  • As soon as you (or a robot player) had a habit, one of its spotters saw it, Robo trusted that spotter, and its wins shot up.
  • When the habit changed, Robo lost for a few rounds, then adapted: another spotter took over.
  • Against truly random play Robo stays near 33%. That's not Robo being silly: no one can predict a fair dice. Being random is the best defence, and it's surprisingly hard for people!
  • Phone keyboards, video sites and shops use the same idea: they look at what you did before to guess what you'll do next.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is online sequence prediction with a mixture of experts. Every round Robo predicts a distribution over the opponent's next move, plays the best response to the most likely move, then updates.

  • Experts (5): frequency counts over moves (order-0); an order-1 Markov model conditioned on the opponent's last move; an order-2 Markov model conditioned on their last two moves; a win-stay / lose-shift detector that counts the opponent's move relative to their own last move (same / the move that beats it / the move it beats), conditioned on the last result; and a "copies Robo" detector that counts their move relative to Robo's last move.
  • Counts: per context, with exponential forgetting: before each update the context's counts are multiplied by the memory γ (slider, default 0.9, an effective window of about 1/(1−γ) = 10 visits). Predictions use add-0.2 smoothing: p = (c + 0.2) / (n + 0.6).
  • Gating by recent accuracy: each expert keeps an exponentially weighted hit rate a ← 0.85·a + 0.15·hit (hit = 1 if its argmax was right, split on ties). Mixing weights are a softmax we ∝ exp(10·ae), and the prediction is Σ we pe. This is a simple cousin of the Hedge / multiplicative-weights algorithm.
  • Decision: Robo commits beats(argmax p) before seeing the opponent's move (ties broken at random), so it never sees the move it is answering.
  • Measured in Node (seeded, 200 rounds each): against Always-rock, Cycler, Copycat and Sore loser, Robo won 100% of the last 50 rounds; it first hit 80% over a 20-round window right at round 20. Against Truly random, Robo averaged about 33% (32–34% depending on the seed) over 40 runs of 300 rounds. A simulated "human-style" player who avoids repeating a move 85% of the time lost about 39% of rounds.
  • Game theory: the Nash equilibrium of rock-paper-scissors is the uniform mixed strategy. Against it, every strategy, however clever, has expected win rate 1/3. Any detectable bias, even a subtle one like avoiding repeats, is exploitable, which is why RPS bots in online competitions use ensembles of exactly these kinds of predictors.
  • Peeking shows Robo's committed move. Knowing a model's prediction lets you exploit it, a small taste of adversarial attacks on predictors.
