How it works

Try every question

The tree tries every possible straight line: "left or right of here?", "above or below here?"

Pick the tidiest split

It keeps the question that leaves each side the least mixed up.

Ask again inside

Each side gets its own new question, until every box is tidy or we run out of questions.

Forest votes

Many slightly different trees each guess. The most popular answer wins — fewer silly mistakes!

What just happened?

  • The tree split the map with yes/no questions, one at a time.
  • Each question was chosen to make the boxes less mixed.
  • A very deep tree gets every practice shape right but trips on new test shapes — it memorised instead of learning.
  • A forest of different trees voting together makes smoother, safer guesses.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is a CART decision tree and a random forest on two features.

  • Each split is an axis-aligned threshold chosen to minimise weighted Gini impurity G = 1 − Σ pₖ² of the two children ("how mixed is this box"). The tree here grows best-first: each click splits the leaf with the biggest impurity reduction.
  • Max depth limits complexity. Deep trees have low bias but high variance: practice (training) accuracy → 100% while test accuracy drops — overfitting.
  • A random forest trains each tree on a bootstrap sample (draw n points with replacement) and considers a random feature subset at each split (here: one of the two axes). Predictions average the trees' class probabilities (bagging), which cuts variance.
  • The small multiples show each tree's own decision map; the big map is the averaged vote.
