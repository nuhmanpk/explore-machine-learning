What just happened?

  • The picture is a graph: dots are people, lines are friendships. A force layout pulls friends together and pushes everyone else apart, so clumps appear by themselves.
  • Groups were found by label passing. Everybody starts with their own label and keeps copying the label most of their friends have, until nothing changes.
  • Suggestions come from shared friends. If you and Sam both know Ava, Ben and Cleo, you probably belong together.
  • When you add or remove a friendship, everything is worked out again from the new picture.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The town is an undirected graph G = (V, E) drawn with a force-directed layout (Fruchterman-Reingold style): nodes repel like charges, edges act as springs, a weak gravity keeps everything in view.

  • Community detection uses asynchronous label propagation (Raghavan et al. 2007): visit nodes in random order and adopt the most common label among neighbours, with random tie-breaks. It is very fast but non-deterministic; we fix the seed so results are repeatable. Louvain and Leiden optimise modularity instead and are more robust.
  • Link prediction with common neighbours scores a pair by |N(u) ∩ N(v)|. The Jaccard version normalises by the size of the union: |N(u) ∩ N(v)| / |N(u) ∪ N(v)|, so people with huge friend lists do not win just by being popular.
  • Real systems add more signals (Adamic-Adar weights rare shared friends more, plus profile and activity data) and learn the weights with a model. The idea that friends of friends become friends is called triadic closure.
  • Suggestions can create filter bubbles: recommending only people already close to your group strengthens the clumps.

Further reading: Easley and Kleinberg, Networks, Crowds, and Markets · Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg, The link-prediction problem for social networks (2007).
