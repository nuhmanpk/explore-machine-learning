What just happened?

  • Nobody told the robot what "action" or "funny" means. It only saw stars.
  • It found kids whose stars looked like yours and borrowed their opinions.
  • The taste map showed groups of kids and movies appearing all by themselves.
  • It checks itself by hiding ratings it already knows. More kids joining makes the guesses better.
  • It struggles with cheaters, brand-new movies, odd tastes and when few ratings exist.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

This is collaborative filtering: recommending from patterns in a user-item rating matrix with many missing entries, with no information about the movies themselves. The 20 viewers and 12 movies are synthetic: three hidden taste groups plus noise, with about a third of ratings missing.

  • User-based CF: similarity between two viewers is computed on movies both rated. "Compare tastes" is the Pearson / mean-centred cosine similarity (each person's average is subtracted first); "Compare raw stars" is plain cosine similarity, which is high for almost everyone because ratings are all positive. The prediction is your mean plus the similarity-weighted average of each neighbour's deviation from their own mean, using the top-k neighbours. Similarities with few shared movies are shrunk.
  • Matrix factorisation: rating ≈ μ + bᵤ + bᵢ + pᵤ·qᵢ with rank 2, trained by stochastic gradient descent with L2 regularisation. The 2-D vectors are latent factors, which is why the taste map can be drawn. The axes themselves have no fixed meaning. Both charts use hold-out ratings the model never trained on (RMSE); the training error keeps falling while hold-out error can stall or rise, which is overfitting.
  • Cheating / no signal: constant ratings have zero variance after centring, so the similarity is undefined and the model falls back to the crowd's average, a popularity baseline.
  • Cold start: an item or user with no ratings has no similarity to use. Real systems fall back to popularity or content features (genre, text) and hybrid models.
  • Sparsity: fewer observed ratings means fewer co-rated items, noisier similarities and worse error.
  • Filter bubbles: recommending only what your twins liked narrows what you see, and what is shown shapes what gets rated next (a feedback loop). Real systems add exploration and diversity.

Further reading: Koren, Bell and Volinsky, "Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems" (2009) · the Netflix Prize.
