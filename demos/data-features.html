What just happened?

  • With only x and y, the robot can cut the picture with a straight line. A bullseye or a checkerboard cannot be split by one line, so it guesses.
  • The clue distance from the centre turns the bullseye into a simple one-number question: is it small or big? The clue x times y does the same for the corners.
  • Making new clues from the ones you have is called feature engineering. Good clues beat a smarter robot.
  • Random junk clues carry no information, but the robot can still use them to memorise the training dots. Held-out accuracy drops, especially with few training points.
  • More training points make junk less harmful, but the best answer is still: use fewer, better clues.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The model is logistic regression trained by batch gradient descent on standardised features with a small L2 penalty (λ = 0.001). Because it is linear in its inputs, it can only draw a straight boundary in feature space. With the right derived feature the boundary becomes curved in the original (x, y) picture.

  • Bullseye: label is x² + y² < 0.75². The feature r = √(x²+y²) (or x² plus y² together) makes it linearly separable. Corners: label is x·y > 0, and the single interaction feature x·y separates it perfectly apart from 3% label noise.
  • Importance here is each clue's share of the total absolute weight. Features are standardised so weights are comparable. This is a simple measure; correlated clues share credit in confusing ways.
  • The four junk features are uniform random numbers independent of the label. With n training points and d features, a model can overfit by fitting noise. Held-out accuracy (400 fresh points) exposes it; training accuracy usually does not.
  • Related ideas: polynomial features, the kernel trick (do this implicitly), neural networks (learn their own features), and feature selection (L1 penalty, mutual information, permutation importance).
