What just happened?

  • Flat, skewed or two-humped, the source can look any way. The averages of many draws still form the same bell shape.
  • One average is noisy. Averages of bigger groups are steadier: the bell gets narrower as the group grows.
  • That is why a poll of 1,000 people tells you a lot even though the country has millions.
  • A confidence interval says "the truth is probably in here", and the claim really comes true about 95 times out of 100.
  • The bootstrap gets an interval with no formula: pretend your sample is the whole world and redraw from it again and again.
For grown-ups: what's really going on

The central limit theorem: for independent draws with finite mean μ and variance σ², the sample mean of n draws is approximately Normal(μ, σ² / n) for large n, whatever the source. The spread of the averages (the standard error) is σ / √n, the same 1/√n as in the Monte Carlo lesson.

  • "Lopsided" is the sample skewness. A skewed source with skewness γ gives averages with skewness about γ / √n, so the lopsidedness shrinks as n grows. Heavy-tailed sources (infinite variance) break the theorem.
  • The formula interval is x̄ ± 1.96 · s / √n. Its claim is about the procedure: over many samples about 95% of such intervals contain μ. It does not say "95% chance for this one interval". At small n with a skewed source it under-covers slightly, because s is itself noisy.
  • The bootstrap (Efron, 1979) resamples the observed sample with replacement 1,000 times (here 300 in the 200-trial test), and takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled means (percentile interval). It needs no formula, which helps for medians, ratios and other messy statistics.
  • The bell curve overlay is the CLT prediction using the source's true mean and sd, measured from a fixed sample of 60,000 draws.
