    What's happening inside

    Challenges

    First search: type any word and get results.
    Word endings: search something like "running dogs" so that matching endings changes which pages are found.
    Tiny words: search "the of a" so that ignoring tiny words changes the results.
    Popularity shuffle: mix in PageRank until a different page becomes number one. Try "park".

    What just happened?

    • Every page became a list of numbers: one number per word. Common words like "the" get tiny numbers, rare words get big ones.
    • Your search became a list of numbers in the same way. A page ranks high when its numbers point the same way as yours.
    • The coloured chips show each word's share of a page's score, so you can always see why something ranked where it did.
    • Ignoring tiny words and matching word endings changes which pages count as a match.
    • Mixing in popularity lets pages that many others link to climb, even if they match your words a little less.
    For grown-ups: what's really going on

    This is a miniature vector space model retrieval system. Each document is a sparse vector of TF-IDF weights and the query is scored by cosine similarity.

    • Term frequency: tf = 1 + ln(count) (sublinear, so repeating a word 10 times is not 10 times better).
    • Inverse document frequency: idf = ln((N + 1) / (df + 1)) + 1. A word in nearly every document says little; a rare word is a strong clue.
    • Both vectors are L2-normalised, so the cosine is just the dot product. It is the sum over query words of qw · dw, which is exactly the chip shown for each word. That is why this model is easy to explain.
    • Stop words are dropped, and a crude stemmer strips endings (real engines use Porter or lemmatisers, and it makes mistakes such as merging unrelated words).
    • The final score is (1 − m) · cosine + m · popularity, where popularity is PageRank (scaled to 0 to 1) over invisible "see also" links between the pages. Only pages that match at least one query word are ranked.
    • Real engines add inverted indexes for speed, BM25 (a refined TF-IDF), hundreds of other signals, and learned neural re-rankers. They also match meaning, not just words, which this model cannot do: "puppy" will never find "dog".

    Further reading: Manning, Raghavan and Schütze, Introduction to Information Retrieval, ch. 6 · Robertson and Zaragoza, The probabilistic relevance framework: BM25 and beyond.
